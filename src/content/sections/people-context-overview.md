If there is one factor that has shaped the difficulty of this programme more than any other, it is this: the gap between the direction set by senior leadership and the understanding and adoption demonstrated by Greg and Darren, ESco's two most senior technical staff.

This is not a peripheral observation. It is the root cause of the majority of technical friction encountered from Phase 3 onwards.

## The intent

From the outset, the programme was designed to bring the development team with it. Greg and Darren were not sidelined — they were central to the plan. The senior leadership team invested heavily in sharing context: the architecture principles, the strategic direction, the rationale behind every major decision. The intent was to empower them to grow into the new model, to become active participants in designing ESco's technical future rather than passive recipients of instruction.

That intent was genuine and sustained. It was also, ultimately, unrealised.

## The pattern

Despite the volume of context shared with them, Greg and Darren continued to operate in ways that reflected the old model. They remained gatekeepers — of existing systems, of inherited approaches, of their own technical domain — rather than making the shift toward strategic thinking that the programme required. Engagement with senior leadership was inconsistent. Messages absorbed in one conversation did not reliably carry forward into the next. Decisions made collectively were not always actioned in the spirit in which they were made.

Both Greg and Darren hold senior positions within the business. But senior position and senior leadership are not the same thing. What was consistently missing was the initiative, the ownership, and the joined-up thinking that those positions imply. The mixed signals that came back to the leadership team — different interpretations, unclear status on key workstreams, uncertainty about whether strategic intent had been understood — became a compounding source of friction that slowed the programme at multiple points.

## The role change: October 2025

In October 2025, a deliberate decision was made to clarify the mandate. Greg and Darren were moved out of developer roles entirely. Their new title and function was the **Solutions Team**: not builders of software, but finders and configurers of the right tools and systems to solve business problems. ESco's direction of travel — away from bespoke development and toward a composable, integration-led architecture — made this a logical and necessary evolution.

The change was made with clear intent and communicated directly. It was not punitive. It was an attempt to remove the identity friction that had been slowing them down and give them a well-defined role in the new model.

## What remained

The honest assessment is that the pattern has persisted. The volume of context provided to Greg and Darren over the life of this programme has been substantial. The direction has been unambiguous. But the depth of adoption — the genuine internalisation of what ESco is trying to become and why — has remained incomplete.

For anyone reading this documentation and trying to understand why certain decisions took longer than expected, why some architectural conversations had to be had more than once, or why senior leadership has had to carry more of the technical strategic load than anticipated: this is the explanation. Not a failure of technology. Not a failure of strategy. A persistent gap between the people leading the change and the people who needed to change with it.
